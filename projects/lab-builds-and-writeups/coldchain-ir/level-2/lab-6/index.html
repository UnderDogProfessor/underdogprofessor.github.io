COLD CHAIN Lab 6: one sign-in, written down on three machines

The first Level 2 lab: trace one normal sign-in and one shared folder through three machines' logs on a small Windows domain. My run found a sign-in type I didn't expect, and a filter that missed every sign-in.

Level
2, A small company
Time
About 90 minutes
Lab network
Windows Server 2025 domain controller and file server, three Windows 11 PCs, in VMware Workstation
Evidence
Security logs from three machines, events 4624, 4768, 4769, 4625 and 4771
Tools
Event Viewer, EvtxECmd and Timeline Explorer

What this is

This is the first lab in Level 2 of COLD CHAIN, a set of hands-on incident response labs I’m building in public around a made-up company, Rimeport Cold Logistics. Level 1 used one Windows 11 virtual machine. Level 2 gives Rimeport a small network: a domain controller (DC01), a file server (FS01), Dana’s computer from Level 1 and two more PCs, on a private VMware network with no way out.

Building it took me from October 29 to November 4, and it didn’t go smoothly. On my Windows Server 2025, DNS refused to create a zone ending in .example (error 123), so the domain became corp.rimeport.internal. Everything I tripped over is now in the setup guide.

The story: before anything goes wrong, the team lead wants to know what a normal morning looks like. When Dana signs in and opens the dispatch folder, which machines write it down, and what does each one see?

What the lab asks

You make a normal morning: Dana signs in at her PC and opens a shared folder on FS01, then Owen does the same from his. Then you read each machine’s own Security log in Event Viewer: her PC for the sign-in (4624), DC01 for the Kerberos tickets it hands out (4768 and 4769), and FS01 for her network sign-in.

Then you do it again the way responders usually do: collect the three logs, fingerprint each one with SHA-256, and read them as one timeline with Eric Zimmerman’s EvtxECmd and Timeline Explorer.

What I found

  • Three machines wrote down one sign-in. Dana signed in at 8:41:29 PM. Her PC wrote it, and one second later DC01 handed her a first ticket, from her PC’s address. When she opened the Dispatch folder at 8:42:49 PM, DC01 wrote a ticket for FS01, and FS01 wrote a network sign-in (Logon Type 3) from her PC in the same second. None of them wrote down which file she opened.
  • Her sign-in wasn’t the type I expected. I expected Logon Type 2, someone at the keyboard. Her PC wrote a Type 11 and a Type 7 in the same second. Microsoft describes 11 as signing in with saved credentials without asking the domain controller, yet DC01 gave her a ticket a second later. My own admin account’s sign-ins on the same PC were ordinary Type 2 pairs. I don’t know yet why hers differ.
  • She got four kinds of ticket, and I screenshotted the wrong one. Tickets for her own PC, for DC01, for krbtgt and for FS01. Only the FS01 one matches opening the folder. My first pick was the one for her PC.
  • My first timeline filter missed every sign-in. Filtering Timeline Explorer’s User Name column for Dana showed 248 rows, all from her PC, and none of her sign-ins or tickets. EvtxECmd puts her name in Payload Data1 for those. Event Viewer had found them all. The two tools disagreeing taught me more about the second one than agreement would have.
  • Timeline Explorer showed UTC. Her 8:41:29 PM sign-in appeared as 01:41:29 the next day.
  • I was the noisiest account on the network. 342 records mention my own account in one hour, including five wrong passwords, each leaving a 4625 on the PC and a 4771 on DC01. Without my notes, that hour looks like someone guessing an admin password and then moving between machines.

What I missed, and what changed because of my run

I made plenty of mistakes, and most of them are in the logs. I signed in to the domain controller as Administrator instead of my own account. I typed RIMEPORT/dana.reyes with a forward slash, and the log kept it word for word. I mistyped my own password five times. Two machines were on Pacific time while the rest used Eastern. And I hashed one export of a log but copied another, so one of my three hashes didn’t match. Off the logs, I’d kept my lab passwords in each VM’s description and forgot them after a break.

The lab and the setup guide changed because of the run. Every sign-in step now names the machine and the account. Setup sets and checks the time zone on every machine and starts with a password list. The answers describe the Type 11 and 7 sign-in, the ticket question asks for the one that matches the folder, and Timeline Explorer now filters Payload Data1. The Level 2 README lists the lessons from the build.

Try it

The setup guide, the lab and my notes are on GitHub, with my screenshots and the three Security logs I collected, so you can open the real records yourself. Do the lab before reading my notes. Your times will differ.
