COLD CHAIN Lab 5: writing one page about an incident

The last Level 1 lab: replay a password guess, a new admin account and a startup task as one incident, then write it up for a reader outside IT. My first draft said we were 100% confident. The log said otherwise.

What this is

This is the fifth and last lab in Level 1 of COLD CHAIN, a set of hands-on incident response labs I’m building in public around a made-up company, Rimeport Cold Logistics. It uses the same Windows 11 virtual machine as Lab 4.

The story: over one morning, someone guessed at Dana’s password, a new admin account appeared, and something was set to run every time she signs in. The IT manager isn’t technical and wants one page: what happened, how sure are we, and what should we do now?

What the lab asks

You replay Labs 2 to 4 in one go, so the times tell one story: three wrong passwords, then the right one, then a new account made an administrator, then a scheduled task that runs at every sign-in. You export the evidence, then fill in a one-page template: what happened in plain words, how sure we are, a timeline where every line points to its record, what we can’t tell, and what we recommend.

What I found

  • My times matched the log to the minute. Writing each time as I went was what I missed in Lab 4, and this time the page practically wrote its own timeline: guesses at 12:57, 12:58 and 1:01, the unlock at 1:02, the account at 1:03, the task at 1:05.
  • The log didn’t connect the guesser to the account. The unlock after the wrong passwords wrote its own sign-in records. But the new account, the admin add and the task were all credited to the sign-in from 12:52, before any guessing, even from a Terminal I opened after unlocking. The timing lines up. The records don’t link them.
  • Windows kept busy in the background. While I was typing wrong passwords, Windows made and deleted its own tasks, and OneDrive re-created its update task, all with Dana’s account as the Subject.
  • Writing it took longer than finding it. Saying it without event IDs, and without claiming more than the log shows, was the hard part.

What I missed, and what changed because of my run

My first draft said we were “100% confident on what has happened”. The log proves what Dana’s account did, not who did it, and it can’t tie the guessing to the account. My draft also said the task gives admin rights to anyone, which isn’t what “highest privileges” means, and it used Eastern Standard Time when daylight time was still on. I didn’t save Windows’ own record of when the task ran, and my screenshot of the evidence list caught the filter box instead.

The lab changed because of the run. It now asks which sign-in did the work, its example page no longer claims one session did everything, and the rules for the page warn against “100% sure” and missing daylight time. It also asks for screenshots of the commands, and a cleanup command that had lost its backslashes is fixed.

That’s Level 1 done: normal sign-ins, guessing, a new admin account, something that survives a restart, and one page that puts it together.

Try it

The lab is on GitHub, and so are my notes, my finished page, my screenshots and my exported .evtx logs. Write your own page before reading mine.
