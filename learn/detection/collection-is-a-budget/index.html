Collection is a budget and rules compete for it

Every field you keep costs money to move, store and search. Deciding what not to collect is a detection decision, and it is usually made by accident.

Collection feels like a technical setting. It is a spending decision, and somebody is making it whether or not that person is you.

Every field has a price paid three times: to move it, to store it for the retention period, and to search it when someone asks. Multiply by the number of hosts and the arithmetic stops being abstract quickly.

This is why verbosity gets turned down. A source is noticed for being large, a setting gets dialled back, and nothing bad appears to happen, because the detections that depended on those fields simply stop matching and report nothing.

The problem is not that the trade exists. Something has to give, and blanket maximum verbosity everywhere is neither affordable nor useful.

The problem is who makes the trade and with what information. Cuts made on volume alone remove the cheapest useful data first, because value and size are unrelated. A handful of small, rare, high-signal events cost almost nothing and carry most of what you would want during an incident.

So the decision needs the rule set in the room.

Find which source your detections read from most often. Then find what it costs you per month. Knowing both numbers is the whole point.

Why. The cost of losing a source is not its volume, it is the detections and investigations that depend on it, and only the people who know the rule set can price that. The platform team has the consumption figures and no visibility of which fields carry detection value, which is why cuts made on volume alone remove the cheapest useful data first. Application owners understand their logs and not what security does with them. Budget ownership decides how much has to go, never which part.
