Has this fired before is the first question

An alert with no history behind it makes the analyst rebuild context by hand, every time, for something that may be a weekly occurrence.

An analyst opens an alert. Before anything else, they want to know whether this is unusual here.

Most alerts cannot answer that. They describe one moment with no reference to any other moment, so the analyst rebuilds the context by hand: search the rule name, filter to the host, scan the dates, form an impression. Five minutes, repeated for every alert, to learn something the platform already knows.

Prevalence is the cheapest enrichment available and one of the most valuable. Two numbers do most of the work. How many times this rule has fired on this entity, and when it last did.

First time ever on this host changes the posture immediately. Two hundredth time this quarter, all closed benign, changes it the other way and is a tuning signal nobody had to go looking for.

There is a second benefit. Prevalence makes rare events visible as rare, which is the property you want from detections aimed at behaviour rather than signatures. A rule that fires constantly everywhere is telling you about your estate. A rule that fires once, somewhere new, is telling you about an event.

Open your last ten alerts. Count how many told you whether they had fired on that entity before.

Why. Prevalence answers the question an analyst actually opens with, which is whether this is normal here. First time on this host at three in the morning and four hundredth time this month are different situations that the alert otherwise presents identically. The raw event is genuinely useful and is usually one click away already. Technique mapping serves reporting and coverage work rather than the decision in front of you, and the author matters when the rule needs changing, not when it fires.
