Your log sources belong to other teams

The systems your detections read from are run by people with their own roadmaps, and none of those roadmaps has your rules written on it.

Detection engineering depends on data produced by systems you do not run.

The firewall belongs to the network team. The identity logs belong to whoever owns the directory. Application logs belong to the developers who wrote the application, and the cloud audit trail belongs to whoever holds the billing account. Each of those teams has a roadmap, and your rules are not on it.

So changes land without warning. A version upgrade renames fields. A cost review turns down log verbosity. A migration moves a service and its logging with it. Somebody disables a noisy debug category that happened to be the only place a useful event appeared.

None of that is carelessness. Nobody told them they were operating a security control, because on their side they are not.

Two things help, and both are relationship work rather than engineering. Know who owns each source you depend on, by name, before you need them. And get log format into the change process as an interface, so a team that is about to change it has one prompt asking who consumes this.

List your five most relied-upon sources. Write the owning team beside each. If you cannot fill in more than three, that is this week’s work.

An application team plans a platform upgrade that changes its log format. How does security usually find out?

After the fact, when a parser breaks or a detection stops matching

Why. Log format is rarely treated as an interface in change processes, so it changes without anybody thinking they have affected a consumer. The other three describe controls that genuinely exist in some organisations and are the exception rather than the norm, and each depends on somebody having already connected that upgrade to your detections. Where none of that exists, the first signal is a broken parser or a silent rule, which is the expensive way to learn it.
