A device can be an identity

A registered device holds a key of its own, so a policy can demand the machine as well as the person before granting access.

Authentication used to answer one question: who is this. Modern directories answer two, because the device is registered as well and carries a credential of its own.

When a workstation joins or registers with an identity provider, it generates a key pair and keeps the private half. Sign-in then presents two things, the user’s credential and the device’s. A policy can require both, which is what “only managed devices” means once you take the marketing off it.

That is the mechanism behind a control which otherwise sounds like a slogan. A stolen password does not satisfy a policy that also wants a device key, because the key does not leave the machine that generated it.

It also changes what a compliance signal is. The device object carries state: whether the disk is encrypted, whether it is patched, whether management still considers it healthy. A policy reads that state at sign-in, which makes the device object something an attacker would quite like to influence, and makes the people who can mark a device compliant worth knowing about.

Look at your conditional access policies and count how many require a managed or compliant device. Then find out who can set that compliance state.

CHECK YOURSELF

A policy requires a managed device for access to finance applications. An attacker has the finance manager's password and a working MFA code. What stops them?

SHOW THE ANSWER

The device key, which was generated on the manager's machine and cannot be presented from the attacker's

Why. Registration puts a private key on the device, and that key is what the policy is really asking for. The attacker holds both of the user's factors and still cannot produce it, which is the entire value of the control. Compliance is not evaluated ahead of credentials, so the second option inverts the order. The first and fourth describe behaviours that would make the control far weaker than it is, and they are the assumptions worth correcting, because people often treat a device requirement as a one-off check rather than a credential in its own right.
