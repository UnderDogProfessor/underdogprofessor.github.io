The account you excluded from every policy

Emergency access accounts are deliberately exempt from the controls protecting everybody else, which makes them the least defended way into the tenant.

Every conditional access deployment guide tells you to exclude a break-glass account, and the advice is right. A policy that applies to everyone can lock out everyone, including the administrators who would fix it. The exclusion is the way back in.

Then it sits there for three years.

The account is exempt from the multifactor requirement. Exempt from the device compliance rule. Exempt from the location restriction. Exempt from the control that would otherwise stop somebody signing in with a password and nothing else. Everything your identity program spent a year building routes around this one object, on purpose, by design, with a documented reason.

An attacker does not have to defeat a policy that was written to ignore an account.

So the protection has to come from somewhere other than policy. A long random credential nobody memorizes, split so that no single person holds all of it. Hardware-backed authentication where the platform allows it without reintroducing the dependency you were avoiding. Storage that is offline and physically controlled. And above all, alerting on any use whatsoever, because the expected number of sign-ins is zero, and anything above zero is either a rehearsal somebody scheduled or an incident nobody did.

Go and find out whether a sign-in by your break-glass account tonight would page a human, or land quietly in a log nobody reads until Monday.

CHECK YOURSELF

Your tenant keeps two emergency access accounts excluded from every conditional access policy, exactly as the vendor guidance recommends. What follows from that?

SHOW THE ANSWER

They are the least defended path in, so the monitoring on them has to be stronger than the policy they skip

Why. The exclusion is correct and should stay. A policy that applies to everyone can lock out everyone, including the people who would repair it, so a way back in has to exist outside the policy. What follows is that protection for these accounts cannot come from policy and has to come from elsewhere: credential handling, physical control, and alerting. Leaving them disabled defeats the purpose if nobody can enable them during the outage, and a long password alone does nothing against a credential that leaks. The expected number of sign-ins is zero, which makes any sign-in worth waking somebody.
