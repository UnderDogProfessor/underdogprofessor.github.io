Who can reset whose password

The right to reset an account's password is the right to become it, and that right is usually delegated by container rather than by account.

Directory permissions are not only about reading. They include the right to act on another account, and the most useful of those is resetting its password.

If you can reset an account’s password, you can become that account. There is no exploit involved and no credential to steal. It is one supported administrative action, and the only thing standing in the way is that the directory writes it down.

These rights are normally delegated at the container level. A service desk group is given reset rights over an organizational unit and inherits them down to everything inside it. That is sensible, right up until somebody moves a privileged account into that unit, or creates one there, or nests a group into the service desk group for an unrelated reason.

The path that results is invisible from a group membership list. The service desk account is not a member of anything privileged. It holds a permission on an object that is.

This is why attack path tooling exists at all. The interesting routes through a directory are permissions rather than memberships, and permissions are not where people look.

Take your three most privileged accounts and list everyone holding reset rights over each one. Count the people. It is almost always a larger number than you would have guessed.

CHECK YOURSELF

A service desk group holds password reset rights over an organizational unit. Somebody moves the service account that owns your backup agents into that organizational unit. What has changed?

SHOW THE ANSWER

Everyone in the service desk group can now take over that service account by resetting its password

Why. Delegated rights are inherited down the container, so moving an object into it grants those rights over the object. Reset rights do not care whether an account logs on interactively. Automatic reapplication of default permissions is a real mechanism, which is what makes that option tempting, but it covers members of specific protected groups rather than every account you happen to consider important, and a backup service account is usually not one of them. The group's own memberships are beside the point: this privilege is a permission held on an object, which is exactly why it never appears in a membership report.
