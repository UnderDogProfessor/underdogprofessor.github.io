Any user can ask for any service ticket

A domain controller issues a service ticket to anyone holding a valid TGT, and that ticket is encrypted with the service account's password-derived key.

A domain controller does not ask why you want a service ticket. It checks that your ticket-granting ticket is valid, finds the service principal name you asked for, and returns a ticket encrypted with that service account’s key. Whether you are allowed to use the service is decided later, by the service.

So any account holding a TGT can request a ticket for any SPN in the domain. That is not a flaw. It is how the protocol spreads the work out: the controller issues, the service decides.

The consequence is in what the ticket is made of. Its encryption key is derived from the service account’s password. A ticket is a blob the requester can take away and attack offline, at whatever speed their hardware allows, with no failed logons at the domain controller and no lockout counter to trip.

That reframes the risk. For a service account with an SPN, password strength is not one control among several. It is the only thing between a request anybody can make and the plaintext password.

Which is the argument for machine accounts and group managed service accounts. Both carry long random passwords that rotate on their own, so an offline attack has nothing worth chewing on.

List the user accounts in your directory that have an SPN set. For each one, find out when its password last changed and whether a person chose it.

CHECK YOURSELF

A service account with an SPN has a twelve character password, and the domain locks accounts after five failed attempts. What does the lockout policy contribute to protecting that password?

SHOW THE ANSWER

Nothing, because the guessing happens offline against an issued ticket and never reaches a domain controller

Why. Lockout counts failed authentications at the domain controller, and here there are none. The single request that was made succeeded, because the controller was willing to issue the ticket, and everything after that happens on hardware the attacker owns. The service never sees an attempt either, since nothing is presented to it until the password is already known. That is why length and randomness carry the whole load for an account with an SPN: the controls that normally slow guessing down all sit on a path the attacker is not using.
