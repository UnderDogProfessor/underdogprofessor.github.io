An account with no person behind it

A service account cannot answer an MFA prompt, cannot notice a phishing page and cannot be asked to confirm anything, so every control that assumes a human is absent.

Most identity controls quietly assume somebody is there. MFA asks a question. A risky sign-in prompt waits for confirmation. Phishing-resistant authentication assumes an attacker has to fool a person.

A service account has nobody to fool and nobody to ask. It authenticates at three in the morning because that is when the job runs, from a host nobody is sitting at, with a credential stored in a file.

So the usual toolkit does not transfer. Bolting MFA onto one either breaks the service or creates a shared second factor that four people can use, which is a worse position than you started in.

What does transfer is everything that does not need a human. Scope the account to the minimum it genuinely needs, which is usually far less than it was given on the day somebody wanted the job to work. Use managed identities or group managed service accounts where the platform rotates the secret for you. Where you cannot, make the credential long and random and rotate it on a schedule somebody owns. Restrict where it is allowed to authenticate from.

Then watch it, because a service account’s behavior is the most predictable in your estate. Same hosts, same hours, same targets. Anything else is worth an alert.

Pick your three most privileged service accounts and write down who owns each.

CHECK YOURSELF

You are asked to bring service accounts up to the same standard as user accounts, starting with multifactor authentication. What is the right first move?

SHOW THE ANSWER

Exclude them from MFA policy and compensate with scope, rotation and source restrictions

Why. MFA asks a question only a person can answer, so applying it to a non-interactive account either breaks the service or produces a shared second factor that several people hold, which is worse than none. The honest approach is to accept that this control does not apply and spend the effort where it does: least privilege on what the account can reach, managed or long random credentials rotated automatically, and restrictions on where it may authenticate from. Converting them to user accounts changes the label and none of the facts.
