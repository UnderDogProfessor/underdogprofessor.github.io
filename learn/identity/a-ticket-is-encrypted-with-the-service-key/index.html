A service ticket is encrypted with the service account's key

Issuing a Kerberos ticket is not an authorization decision, and the ticket that comes back is protected only by the service account's password.

Ask a domain controller for a ticket to a service and it gives you one. It does not check whether you are allowed to use that service. Authorization is the service’s job, and it happens later.

The ticket comes back encrypted with a key derived from the service account’s password. That is how the service knows the ticket is genuine: only it holds the key to open it.

Now hold those two facts together. Any authenticated user can request a ticket for any service in the domain, and what arrives is material encrypted under a service account’s password-derived key.

That material can be attacked offline. Nothing is sent to the service, no authentication fails, and no lockout counter moves. The only thing standing in the way is how good the password is.

Which is why service account passwords are a different problem from user passwords. A user password is shielded by lockout thresholds and by somebody eventually noticing a run of failed logons. A service account’s key is sitting inside every ticket ever issued for it, and the account is often privileged and rarely rotated.

Two things close the gap. A Group Managed Service Account gets a long random password that the domain rotates on its own, which puts offline attack out of practical reach. Where a gMSA will not fit, a long random password does the same job by hand.

Go and look at your service accounts. Ask which ones have a password a person chose. Start there.

CHECK YOURSELF

Your domain has forty service accounts and you want to reduce exposure to offline attacks against Kerberos service tickets. Which change does the most?

SHOW THE ANSWER

Move the accounts to Group Managed Service Accounts, or give them long random passwords

Why. The attack runs offline, against a ticket the attacker already holds, so nothing about it touches a domain controller a second time. Lockout counters never move and ticket lifetime is irrelevant, because the encrypted material stays attackable long after the ticket would have expired. Restricting who may request a ticket is not something Kerberos offers, since issuing one is deliberately not an authorization decision. What is left is making the key unguessable, which is precisely what a gMSA password or a long random password does.
