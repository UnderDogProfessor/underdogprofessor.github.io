Vulnerability/Defensive guide/October 8, 2026

The newest one is from 2023

CISA added five vulnerabilities to KEV on October 8, 2026. The newest is from 2023, two are from 2015, and four require forensic triage.

SHREYAS LIPARE · GCIH · CEH MASTER

Anyone whose vulnerability management programme is driven by advisory dates, and anyone who ingests the KEV feed into a ticket queue.

Researched and drafted with AI assistance, then reviewed and edited by Shreyas Lipare before publication. Every source below was checked against the original.

CISA added five vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog today. The newest of them was published in 2023.

Two are from 2015. One of those is a ProFTPD flaw in the mod_copy module that Debian fixed on May 19, 2015, the day after it was published [11][4]. It has been in the catalog for a few hours. It is due for remediation on October 11.

I went looking for what made the batch unusual, because a day where nothing added is current is rare. Since the catalog began carrying a forensic triage flag on July 1, 2026, entries have been added on 46 separate days. Two of those days contained nothing newer than 2023: July 13, which was a single CVE from 2008, and today [1].

What happened

Catalog version 2026.10.08 released at 20:09 UTC with five new entries, all carrying a remediation date of October 11, 2026 [1]. Here they are with the numbers that usually drive a decision, and the one that does not.

CVE Product Published CVSS 3.1 EPSS Forensic triage
CVE-2015-3306 ProFTPD 1.3.5 mod_copy May 18, 2015 10.0 96.75% (99.9th) Yes
CVE-2015-5477 ISC BIND 9.x named July 29, 2015 7.5 91.28% (99.8th) No
CVE-2016-3081 Apache Struts 2.3.x April 26, 2016 8.1 93.35% (99.8th) Yes
CVE-2021-3199 ONLYOFFICE Docs before 5.6.3 January 26, 2021 9.8 8.21% (94.8th) Yes
CVE-2023-22894 Strapi through 4.5.5 April 19, 2023 4.9 1.66% (75.9th) Yes

Scores and vectors are from NVD [4][5][6][7][8]. Probabilities are the EPSS scores dated October 8, 2026 [9]. The triage column is a field in the KEV JSON itself [1].

Read the last two columns together and the ordering is upside down. The entry with the lowest severity in the batch, a 4.9 medium in Strapi that needs admin panel access to begin with, is flagged for forensic triage. The entry sitting at the 99.8th EPSS percentile, the BIND bug, is not.

One more thing about today, which I want to be careful with because it may have resolved by the time you read this. When I checked, CISA’s cybersecurity advisories feed carried no alert announcing these five. The most recent KEV alert in it was dated October 4. The JSON feed had the entries; the announcement channel did not. If your KEV process is driven by the alerts RSS or by a news aggregator reading it, today is a day you would have missed.

Why this matters

Almost every vulnerability management programme I have seen is organised around newness. The intake is an advisory feed. The triage is a CVSS score attached to a recent CVE. The reporting cycle is monthly and it measures how fast this month’s patches went out.

Today’s batch is a direct argument against that shape, because KEV is not an advisory feed. It is a record of what is being exploited in the present, and the present apparently includes an eleven-year-old FTP server bug.

The EPSS column is the part that should land hardest. ProFTPD sits at 96.75 percent, which puts it in the top tenth of one percent of all scored CVEs [9]. Struts and BIND are both above 91 percent. Those are not neglected curiosities that nobody remembers how to exploit. They are among the most reliably exploited flaws in the entire dataset, and they have been for years, quietly, while attention went to whatever shipped last Tuesday.

There is an honest counterpoint sitting in the same table. EPSS would have told you about three of these long ago and would have been close to useless for the other two: the ONLYOFFICE traversal is at 8.21 percent and the Strapi information disclosure at 1.66 percent, which is unremarkable enough to lose any ranking contest. So the lesson is not that EPSS was right all along. It is that the two feeds disagree about these five, and the disagreement is the signal.

For the practical reader, the question is narrower. Do you run any of these five products anywhere, including on a box somebody stood up in 2017 and nobody has logged into since? Struts 2.3 and ProFTPD 1.3.5 are exactly the kind of software that ends up inside an appliance, a vendor-managed application server, or a build host that predates your asset inventory.

Technical breakdown

The forensicTriage field is new and it is doing real work, so it is worth understanding before October 11.

It appears on every entry in the catalog as a plain Yes or No. Across all 1,739 entries, 78 read Yes, and every one of those was added on or after July 1, 2026 [1]. Of the 109 entries added since that date, 78 are flagged, so this is not a rare designation: inside the current era of the catalog it is the majority case at roughly 72 percent.

The requirement behind the flag comes from BOD 26-04, issued June 10, 2026, which supersedes BOD 19-02 and BOD 22-01 and binds federal civilian executive branch agencies [2]. The directive’s own wording for flagged items is that the agency must complete remediation or mitigation within three days and “carry out a forensic triage of the asset to assess whether the system is compromised” [2].

The implementation guidance is where it gets operationally awkward [3]. It is careful to say that the step timings are recommended practice and that “the requirement of BOD 26-04 is that an adequate forensic triage analysis is performed”. But look at the order it recommends:

  1. Scoping, targeted within two hours of the KEV addition.
  2. Preserve and collect evidence, 2 to 24 hours, prioritising volatile data.
  3. Critical patching and stabilisation, 2 to 24 hours.
  4. Contain and control, 6 to 24 hours.
  5. Triage analysis, 24 to 48 hours.
  6. Escalation decision and a written triage report, 48 to 72 hours.

Patching is step three. Evidence collection is step two. The guidance states the reason without hedging: “Do not alter or remediate systems prior to evidence/artifact collection when possible”, and “patching may jeopardize the availability of artifacts” [3]. It makes the same point about containment, warning that “premature containment can destroy vital evidence”.

That is a genuine collision with how most teams behave under a three-day clock, and it is the single most useful thing in the directive. The reflex when a KEV entry lands on an internet-facing box is to patch it tonight. For a flagged entry, patching it tonight without collecting anything first is the one sequence that satisfies the deadline and destroys the answer.

What predicts the flag, and what I could not make predict it

I assumed the flag tracked impact type, because today’s batch fits that reading perfectly. The BIND entry is the only unflagged one, and its CVSS vector is AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:N/I:N/A:H [5]: no confidentiality impact, no integrity impact, availability only. A crashed daemon cannot have left anything behind, so there is nothing to triage for. The four flagged entries all carry confidentiality or integrity impact, including the 4.9 Strapi one at C:H/I:N/A:N [8].

That reading survived its first test. I pulled the eight entries in the current era whose descriptions mention denial of service and found six unflagged and two flagged, which looked like a problem until I opened the two exceptions. Both the Citrix NetScaler entry and the Linux kernel watch_queue entry carry C:H/I:H/A:H: they mention denial of service as one possible outcome among several, and my keyword match had miscategorised them.

Then it broke. Of the 31 unflagged entries added since July, plenty have obvious confidentiality or integrity impact: a WordPress Core SQL injection, a Chromium V8 type confusion leading to code execution, a PaperCut authentication bypass, a FortiOS sensitive information disclosure. Availability-only impact is enough to earn a No, but it is clearly not required for one.

So I tested the other things the catalog makes available. Known ransomware use, end-of-life language in the description, whether the entry carries CISA’s shared-component note, and whether the CVE predates 2025. Every one of those subgroups lands within a few points of the 72 percent base rate. None of them explains anything.

The conclusion I am left with is a negative one, and it is the actionable part: the forensic triage requirement is not derivable from any other field CISA publishes, and neither the directive nor the implementation guidance documents the selection criterion. You cannot infer it from severity, from exploitation probability, from age, or from ransomware association. You have to read the field.

Which brings up the thing to go and check today:

curl -s https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/feeds/known_exploited_vulnerabilities.json \
  | jq -r '.vulnerabilities[]
           | select(.forensicTriage == "Yes")
           | [.dateAdded, .cveID, .dueDate, .vendorProject] | @tsv'

If your KEV ingestion was written before July 2026, it almost certainly maps a fixed set of fields into a ticket and drops the rest. forensicTriage would be among the dropped ones, silently, with no error anywhere. The flag has been arriving for three months.

I have not drawn an attack chain for this post. There is no single adversary sequence here: five unrelated flaws in five unrelated products, with no shared actor and no shared route. Forcing one would produce steps nobody could act on.

What defenders should do Monday morning

Immediate, today. Search your estate for the five products in the table above, and search it in the places your asset inventory is weakest: appliances, vendor-managed application servers, build infrastructure, and anything acquired rather than deployed. For Struts specifically, the fix in S2-032 is to upgrade to 2.3.20.3, 2.3.24.3 or 2.3.28.1, and the documented workaround is to disable Dynamic Method Invocation, which is the setting the flaw depends on [10]. For ProFTPD, the Debian fix has existed since DSA-3263-1 in May 2015 and anything still on 1.3.5 unpatched is more than a decade behind [11].

Near term, this week. Before you patch any flagged asset that is internet-facing, collect from it. Volatile data first, then the logs, and keep a collection log of what you took and when [3]. This is not a counsel of perfection and it does not require a forensics team: an acquisition of memory, process list, network connections, and a copy of the relevant application and authentication logs before the maintenance window is enormously better than nothing, and it is the difference between being able to answer the question later and not.

Then check your intake. Confirm that whatever consumes the KEV feed parses forensicTriage, and that a flagged entry is routed differently from an unflagged one rather than into the same patch queue. Confirm, too, that your process is driven by the JSON feed rather than by the alerts RSS, because today the two did not agree.

Structural, this quarter. Give your vulnerability programme a second intake that is not organised around advisory dates. The KEV catalog and EPSS both describe the present rather than the news cycle, and today they disagreed with each other about three of five entries, which is useful rather than annoying. Then go and establish how far back your logs actually go on your oldest internet-facing systems, because a triage requirement on an eleven-year-old exposure is only answerable to the depth of your retention.

Checklist

  • Do we run ProFTPD, ISC BIND, Apache Struts 2.3.x, ONLYOFFICE Docs, or Strapi anywhere, including inside appliances and vendor-managed systems?
  • Has anything in that list been reachable from the internet on an unpatched version, and for how long?
  • Does our KEV ingestion parse the forensicTriage field, or silently drop it?
  • Is our KEV process driven by the JSON feed or by the alerts RSS?
  • For flagged assets, do we collect evidence before we patch, and do we log what we collected?
  • How far back do logs go on our oldest internet-facing systems?
  • Who decides, and on what evidence, that a flagged asset was not compromised?

The deadline is the easy half

The three-day clock is the part of this that will get attention, and it is the part that matters least. Patching five products is a weekend. Most teams will make it.

What they will not do, because nothing in their process asks them to, is answer the second question. An eleven-year-old flaw on an internet-facing server with a 96 percent exploitation probability has not been quietly safe all this time because nobody noticed it. The honest prior is that somebody did, possibly years ago, and the task is to find out. That is what CISA has started requiring, and the requirement is a sharper piece of policy than the deadline it is attached to, because it is the first time the catalog has said out loud that patching an exploited vulnerability is not the same as recovering from one.

The industry’s answer to a KEV entry has been “patch it” for as long as the catalog has existed. For 78 entries and counting, that answer is now explicitly half of the work, and the guidance says plainly that doing it first destroys the evidence for the other half. I would rather be a week late on step three than clean on step two.

FREQUENTLY ASKED

Does BOD 26-04 apply to me if I am not a US federal agency?
The directive binds federal civilian executive branch agencies only, and it says so. The part that reaches everyone else is the data: the forensicTriage flag now ships in the public KEV JSON that most vulnerability tools already consume, so you inherit the signal whether or not you inherit the obligation. The underlying logic also travels well. If a flaw that could have let somebody read or change your data has been exploitable on an internet-facing box since 2015, patching it and closing the ticket answers the wrong question.
Why would CISA add an eleven-year-old vulnerability to KEV now?
KEV is a record of what is being exploited, not a record of what was disclosed. The catalog entry is evidence that CISA has reliable information of exploitation in the present, regardless of when the flaw was published. For the two 2015 entries here, EPSS has independently had them above 91 percent probability, which means the tooling to exploit them has been widely available and widely used for years.
What does forensic triage actually require?
Per CISA's implementation guidance, the binding requirement is that an adequate forensic triage analysis is performed; the step timings are recommended practice rather than mandate. The sequence it recommends puts evidence collection before patching, prioritises volatile data, requires an evidence collection log, and ends in a written triage report inside 48 to 72 hours. The guidance warns in plain terms that patching may jeopardise the availability of artifacts.
My scanner says we are clean on all five. Am I done?
For the four flagged entries, a clean scan answers whether you are vulnerable now, not whether you were compromised earlier. That is the gap the triage requirement exists to close. If one of these products has been internet-facing on an unpatched version for years, the useful question is what is in the logs you still have, and the uncomfortable follow-up is how far back they go.
Is the triage flag just a severity score under another name?
No, and the numbers say so clearly. The entry with the lowest CVSS in today's batch, a 4.9 medium in Strapi, is flagged. The entry sitting at the 99.8th EPSS percentile, the BIND denial of service, is not. I tested several plausible predictors against the whole catalog and none of them explained the flag, so treat it as an independent field and parse it.
CONFIDENCE AND LIMITATIONS

Every figure here was read from primary feeds on October 8, 2026, after the catalog's own release timestamp of 20:09 UTC that day, and the catalog membership, due dates and triage flags of all five entries were re-checked against the live feed immediately before publication. Three things are worth flagging. First, on both checks there was no CISA alert page announcing these five entries: the KEV JSON feed carried them and the cybersecurity advisories RSS still listed October 4 as its most recent KEV alert, so the announcement may simply have followed later than the data. Second, NVD's own CISA KEV flag still read NO for all five CVEs at the time I pulled their records, which is a mirroring lag in NVD rather than a contradiction of the catalog. Third, I tried and failed to find a rule that predicts the forensicTriage flag from any other published field, and I have reported that failure rather than dressing a partial correlation up as a finding. CISA does not document the selection criterion in either the directive or the implementation guidance, so the honest statement is that it is not derivable and has to be read.
