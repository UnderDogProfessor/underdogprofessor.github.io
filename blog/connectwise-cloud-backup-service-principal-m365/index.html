Cloud/Incident breakdown/October 19, 2026

They tried the password first

One address sprayed four tenants and got nothing. Two days later it signed in to hundreds as an application, and MFA never entered the picture.

SHREYAS LIPARE · GCIH · CEH MASTER

Anyone who administers a Microsoft 365 tenant, and anyone who has approved a vendor application's consent screen without reading what it asked for.

Researched and drafted with AI assistance, then reviewed and edited by Shreyas Lipare before publication. Every source below was checked against the original.

On October 13 and 14, 2026, an address at a hosting provider ran password spraying against four law firm tenants. Huntress reports it got nowhere. No successful user sign-ins [3].

Two days later the same address signed in to hundreds of Microsoft 365 tenants and nothing stopped it [3].

It did not need a password the second time. It authenticated as an application.

What happened

Between 06:13 and 08:34 UTC on October 15, 2026, the ConnectWise Cloud Backup application authenticated into customer tenants from 144.172.64[.]128 and obtained Microsoft Graph tokens [2][3]. Huntress observed tenants being hit in sorted GUID order, seconds apart, which it offers as a tentative pattern useful for prioritizing who to check rather than as a finding [3].

ConnectWise published an advisory on October 19 describing a Cloud Backup service interruption. Its October 17 update is the operative sentence: “We recently identified unauthorized access to application identities within the ConnectWise Cloud Backup environment” [1]. The company says it has not confirmed that any individual partner or customer environment was compromised, and that it has no indicators to date that partner backup data was accessed [1][4].

How the credential was obtained has not been disclosed by anyone.

Microsoft, quoted by BleepingComputer and The Hacker News, called it an “active supply chain attack in which a threat actor compromised the trusted OAuth App of the backup application”, and said it observed “broad successful service principal authentication originating from the IP address” [4][3]. I could not open Microsoft’s own post, so those are carried from the reporting rather than read at source.

The scale depends on who is counting, and the measurements are not the same thing. Huntress confirmed unauthorized sign-ins in 162 tenants as of 19:00 UTC on October 16, a figure later reported as 173 [4][3], and separately described nearly 1,000 tenants seeing sign-ins from that address with around 300 succeeding as of October 19 [2]. Todyl analyzed nine tenants and found sign-in activity with no accounts created and no persistence established [4].

ConnectWise disabled seven application identities on October 17 [4]. As of its October 19 update at 18:50 UTC, three services remain suspended: Cloud Backup, and SaaS Security and Cloud Management as a precaution. The company states that “containment remains in place, with no successful threat activity observed in monitored environments since mitigations initiated October 17, 05:00 UTC”, and that it has not established whether a product vulnerability contributed to the credential exposure [1].

Why this matters

Start with what the application could reach. A cloud backup product for Microsoft 365 needs broad access to do its job, and this one had it: read and write access to mail and attachments, OneDrive and SharePoint files, Teams chats, and the ability to restore data [3]. Microsoft’s assessment is that the attacker was likely abusing permissions customers had already granted, including Directory.ReadWrite.All [2][3].

Nobody was tricked into granting those. They are what the product legitimately requires, and every administrator who approved them made a defensible decision.

The uncomfortable part is what that consent survives. Your MFA policy does not apply, because there is no user to challenge. Conditional access policies written against user sign-ins do not evaluate. Revoking user sessions does nothing, because the application does not hold one. Impossible travel, risky sign-in, device compliance: all of it is machinery built to decide whether a human at a keyboard should be let in, and none of it was in the path.

That is the whole lesson, and the password spraying two days earlier is the proof. The same actor tried the door your controls were built to guard, and your controls held. Then it used a door nobody had thought to guard, and walked through hundreds of tenants in two hours and twenty one minutes.

For the smaller organizations this blog is partly written for, the translation is blunt. If an IT provider manages your Microsoft 365, there is almost certainly a list of third-party applications holding standing permission in your tenant, and almost nobody outside that provider has ever seen it.

Technical breakdown

The sequence, and where it could have been severed:

Three things in that chain deserve more than one line.

The escalation is the part that turns an incident into a problem. An application holding Directory.ReadWrite.All can write to the directory, and writing to the directory includes granting roles. Huntress observed ConnectWise Cloud Management service principals assigning themselves directory roles across nearly 50 monitored tenants, mostly Exchange Administrator, and in at least one tenant the Cloud Management Teams application added itself to Global Administrator [4][3]. The permission that let a backup tool enumerate your users is the same permission that let it promote itself.

The persistence is deliberate and survives the obvious fix. In one partner tenant a script running as Python-urllib/3.12 added new client secrets to two other applications, an MSP management tool and Huntress’s own application [3][4]. Rotating the ConnectWise credential does nothing about those. SpecterOps describes the same pattern: a second application granting itself Global Administrator and seeding secrets elsewhere, specifically so there is a way back after a credential reset [2].

The reconnaissance is invisible, and not because your logging is bad. Huntress reports that directory reads performed through Graph leave no audit trail [3]. You can see the sign-in. You can see role changes and credential additions, which is why those are the detections worth building. What the attacker read while it was in there is not recoverable from your logs, and no licensing tier fixes that.

One timing detail matters for anyone responding now: tokens already issued stay valid until they expire, which Todyl puts at up to ninety minutes, and access genuinely ends only when the vendor rotates the underlying credential [3][4]. Disabling the application in your tenant is still the right first move. It is just not instantaneous.

What defenders should do next

One line in ConnectWise’s advisory decides who owns this work. The company asks partners to review their own Microsoft 365 tenant and every customer tenant they manage, because it cannot see those environments itself [1]. If you are a managed service provider, nobody upstream is checking your customers for you.

Immediate, today. Check whether the ConnectWise Cloud Backup application, object 506e08f9-6e9a-4da4-942b-a099a03644ae, holds consent in your tenant, and disable it if so [2]. Do not delete it: deleting removes the consent record and destroys the evidence of what it was allowed to do [2]. Then search sign-in logs for service principal authentication from the attacker addresses. There are three, not one: the October 19 advisory update added 144.172.64[.]99 and 144.172.64[.]60 alongside the originally published 144.172.64[.]128, found through downstream monitoring [1]. Anyone who built a hunt on the first address over the weekend should rerun it.

Near term, this week. Review every application in your tenant for credentials or certificates added since October 13, 2026, not only the ConnectWise ones, because the persistence step seeded secrets into unrelated trusted applications [2][3]. Then check directory role assignments for any service principal holding one, and ask why for each.

Build the two detections this incident hands you, whether or not you were affected. Alert when a service principal is added to a directory role. Alert when credentials are added to an application registration. Both are low volume in a healthy tenant, which is exactly what makes them worth having.

Structural, this quarter. Produce the list nobody has: every third-party application with standing consent in your tenant, what each can read, and who approved it. Then decide which of them need write access to your directory, because that is the permission that converts a vendor compromise into a tenant compromise. Most backup and reporting tools are asking for more than their function requires, and the consent screen is the only moment anybody looks.

Checklist

  • Does the ConnectWise Cloud Backup application hold consent in our tenant?
  • Have we disabled rather than deleted it?
  • Any service principal sign-ins from 144.172.64[.]128, .99 or .60?
  • Any credentials or certificates added to any application since October 13, 2026?
  • Any service principal currently holding a directory role, and do we know why?
  • Do we alert on service principal role assignment and on app credential additions?
  • Do we have a list of every third-party application consented in our tenant?

Consent is a standing decision

The industry spent a decade teaching people that identity is the perimeter, and then built the entire control set for one kind of identity. Everything an administrator reaches for in an incident, forcing MFA, revoking sessions, blocking a country, disabling a user, assumes a person is on the other end.

Applications are identities too. They authenticate, they hold permissions, they can be granted roles, and in this case one of them promoted itself to Global Administrator in a tenant that had done nothing wrong. They just do not have any of the controls bolted to them, because the controls were specified when the only thing that logged in was a human.

So here is the position I will defend. A consent screen is not a one-time approval, it is a standing grant of access that outlives the person who clicked it, the project that needed it, and in this case the security of the vendor who asked for it. Treat the list of consented applications in your tenant the way you treat the list of domain administrators, because functionally that is closer to what it is. Almost nobody does, and the attacker in this story did not have to work very hard to find that out.

FREQUENTLY ASKED

We have MFA on every account. Why did that not help?
Because nobody signed in as a person. An application authenticates with its own credential, a secret or certificate held by the app rather than by a user, and there is no user, no device and no prompt in that flow. MFA, device compliance and most conditional access policies are written against user sign-ins and simply do not evaluate. This is not a misconfiguration on your side. It is what application authentication is.
We revoked all sessions. Are we clear?
Revoking user sessions does not touch an application's access, because the app does not have a user session. Access tokens already issued stay valid until they expire, which Todyl puts at up to ninety minutes, and the underlying access ends only when the application credential itself is rotated by the vendor or the application is disabled in your tenant. Those are two different actions and only one of them is yours to take.
Should we delete the ConnectWise applications?
Disable them rather than deleting them. Deleting the service principal removes the consent record along with it, which destroys the evidence of what the application was permitted to do and makes any later investigation much harder. SpecterOps makes this point explicitly in its guidance. Disable, investigate, then decide.
How would we even see this in our logs?
Look at sign-in logs for service principal authentication rather than the interactive user sign-ins most dashboards default to, and look for role assignments and credential additions on applications. The uncomfortable part is what you will not see: Huntress reports that directory reads performed through Graph leave no audit trail, so reconnaissance against your directory is invisible by design rather than through any failure of your logging.
We are a small business and our IT provider manages this. What do we ask them?
Three questions. Which third-party applications hold consent in our tenant, and what can each one read? Were any credentials or secrets added to those applications since October 13, 2026? And did any service principal sign in from an address outside the vendor's normal infrastructure. If the answer to the first is that nobody has a list, that is the finding, and it is the same finding whether or not you use this particular backup product.
CONFIDENCE AND LIMITATIONS

This is a live incident, re-verified against ConnectWise's advisory immediately before publication on October 19, 2026, which is where the second and third attacker addresses came from. The numbers are the least settled part of it. Huntress reported 173 tenants with confirmed unauthorized sign-ins and separately described nearly 1,000 tenants seeing sign-ins from the attacker address with roughly 300 successful; Todyl analyzed nine tenants and found sign-in activity with no persistence; ConnectWise says it has not confirmed that any individual partner or customer environment was compromised. Those are different measurements rather than contradictions, and I have attributed each rather than picking one. How the vendor's credential was obtained has not been disclosed by anyone. I was not able to open Microsoft's own write-up, so its quotations here are carried from BleepingComputer and The Hacker News rather than read at source, and I have said so where they appear. Huntress's observation that the targeting followed sorted GUID order is offered by Huntress itself as tentative and useful for prioritization rather than as a finding. The link between the October 13 and 14 password spraying and the October 15 application sign-ins is Huntress's, based on the shared address.
