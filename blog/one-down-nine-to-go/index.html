One down, nine to go

Fifty micro-learnings in forty five days finished the detection track. The pace data says the habit worked differently from how I described it.

SHREYAS LIPARE · GCIH · CEH MASTER

Anyone building a study habit in public, and anyone deciding which part of security to learn next.

Researched and drafted with AI assistance, then reviewed and edited by Shreyas Lipare before publication. Every source below was checked against the original.

The learning section of this site is a grid of ten domains, each complete at fifty pieces. Today one of them is full and nine are empty.

That picture is more honest than the number next to it. Fifty of five hundred sounds like ten percent of a project. What it actually looks like is one bar across and nine flat lines underneath, which is the correct impression.

I want to do two things here. Say what the last forty five days actually measured, including the part that does not flatter me. Then ask you which domain to open next, because I have a preference and I am not confident it is right.

The core issue

Fifty micro-learnings went up between August 16 and September 30, 2026. Forty five days, fifty pieces, which reads like slightly better than one a day.

It was not one a day. It was this:

What the calendar says Count
Days in the period 45
Days something was published 18
Days nothing was published 27
Longest silence 12 days
Entries that arrived on a day with five or more 37 of 50

Three quarters of the output landed on five days. Thirteen entries came from thirteen ordinary single days, and everything else came from sittings where I wrote five or six in a row because the gap had got embarrassing.

I described this publicly as a daily habit. The data describes something else: a burst habit with good intentions attached, running at roughly the right average for entirely the wrong reason.

Why this matters

The averages hid it, which is the part worth generalising.

Fifty over forty five days is 1.1 per day. Every dashboard I could have built would have shown a healthy line. Nothing about the aggregate exposes that the work happened in four sittings and twenty seven days produced nothing at all, and an average that conceals a twelve day silence is not measuring consistency. It is measuring volume and calling it consistency.

This is the same mistake the detection entries keep circling. A number that looks like a rate but hides its distribution tells you almost nothing, which is why one of the pieces argues that a false positive rate with no denominator cannot decide anything. It is easier to see in somebody else’s metrics.

There is a second thing the numbers exposed, and it is worse.

Technical breakdown

The site was built for exactly this problem and I was not using it.

Entries carry a publication timestamp, and a gate compares that timestamp to the current time at build. A future date means the page is not generated for production, so the entry exists in the repository and not on the site. A scheduled job rebuilds the site every morning at 07:05 UTC, which promotes whatever has come due since the last build.

That is a queue. Write six on a Sunday, date them across the following week, and a reader sees one a day without you doing anything. The machinery is about forty lines of logic and a cron entry.

It sat idle for forty five days. Every entry was dated to go live the moment it was written, so the job fired every morning, found nothing due, and rebuilt an unchanged site. The habit was never automated. It was manual work with a scheduler watching.

When I finally queued the last sixteen across sixteen days, the fix took one function call. The problem was never the tooling.

What defenders should do Monday morning

Immediate, today. If you publish, study or practise on a cadence, count the days you actually did it rather than the total you produced. Those are different numbers and only one of them is a habit. Mine were 18 and 50.

Near term, this week. Separate the making from the shipping. Almost every consistency problem I have had dissolves when those two stop being the same event, and most publishing tools already support it. The reason mine did not was not capability, it was that I never queued anything.

Structural, this quarter. Pick what you learn next on evidence rather than on a syllabus. The NICE Framework organises the field into five work role categories, Oversight and Governance, Design and Development, Implementation and Operation, Protection and Defense, and Investigation, and then into work roles beneath them [1]. That is a map of jobs, which is useful for hiring and not much use for deciding what to read on a Tuesday, because a single role assumes knowledge scattered across half a dozen unrelated subjects.

Checklist

  • Count publishing days, not published items.
  • Look at the longest gap, not the average.
  • Queue work ahead of its publication date if your tooling allows it.
  • Pick the next subject you can write from working knowledge, not the next one in a curriculum.
  • Keep a list of things you did not understand during real incidents. That is your syllabus.

So which one next

Detection was first by accident. It is what I do most days, so it was the only track I could write at two hundred words a go without researching first, and that constraint is the entire reason it finished.

The accident turned out well. Detection engineering is where the other nine subjects become visible, because you cannot write a rule without meeting the thing it watches. ATT&CK lists fifteen Enterprise tactics [2], and covering them properly drags you through identity, network protocols, Windows and Linux internals, cloud control planes and application behaviour whether you planned on it or not. Working on the detection entries is what produced most of my list of things I do not know well enough.

It also demonstrated why learning the taxonomy is not the same as learning the subject. The tactic at TA0005 currently carries the title Stealth, with a last modification recorded in May 2026 [3]. Frameworks move. The mechanics underneath them do not.

So here is the question, and I would rather have your answer than my own.

Domain The case for opening it next
Identity and Access Most intrusions I read about are authentication defeated rather than broken, and tokens and sessions are where the confusion is thickest
Incident Response The one with the most immediate career value, and the hardest to write honestly without using employer specifics
Networking Foundations Everything else assumes it, and the assumption is usually wrong
Windows Security Processes, tokens and LSASS underpin half the detection track already
Linux Security Increasingly the thing actually running production, and consistently the thinner half of most people’s knowledge
Cloud Security Where the control plane replaces the host, and where the old mental models quietly stop applying
Application Security The gap between the people who build and the people who defend is widest here
Cryptography in Practice Almost everybody, me included, is working from analogies that break under pressure
Threat Intelligence Confidence language and honest attribution are underrated skills and rarely taught

My instinct says Identity, because it is the subject I most often have to look up mid-incident, and because the detection entries kept bumping into it. My worry is that Networking is the genuinely foundational one and that starting anywhere else is building on sand.

Tell me which, and tell me why. A reason will move me further than a count of votes, and I would rather be argued out of my instinct now than fifty pieces from now.

Postscript, October 9, 2026

I went with Identity, and the first five entries opened that track on October 5, 2026. The argument that decided it was the one in the table above rather than anything clever: almost every incident I had written up that month turned on authentication being defeated rather than broken, and I was tired of looking up the same token and session behaviour mid-write.

The Networking worry has not gone away and I still think it is the better foundational answer. It is now the leading candidate for third.

So the question in this post is settled and the live one has moved along by one. Two tracks open, eight to go, and the same request stands for whichever comes next: give me the reason rather than the vote.

FREQUENTLY ASKED

Why start with detection rather than something more foundational?
Honestly, because it is what I do every day, so it was the one I could write from working knowledge in twenty minutes. That turned out to matter more than the ordering argument. A track you can write on a bad day gets finished; a track you have to research first does not survive week three.
Is fifty short pieces really equivalent to studying a subject?
No, and it is not meant to be. Each one is a single idea a working defender trips over, written in about two hundred words with a question at the end. The value is coverage and recall rather than depth. Depth is what the blog posts are for, and several of them started as a micro-learning that would not fit.
What actually fixed the consistency problem?
Scheduling, not discipline. The site holds an entry back until its publication date and rebuilds daily, so entries written in one sitting can be released one a day. That machinery existed for forty five days before anything was ever queued into it, which is the part I find most embarrassing and most instructive.
How do I pick which domain to learn next in my own case?
Two filters beat any ranking. Which one can you write about, or explain to somebody, from working knowledge today, and which one keeps appearing in incidents you did not understand. The first tells you what you can sustain. The second tells you what is costing you.
Can I vote on what comes next?
The original vote is closed: Identity opened on October 5, 2026, as the postscript records. Eight domains remain and the same invitation stands for the third. Reply here or on LinkedIn with the one you would open next and why, and I will take the argument seriously rather than counting votes.
