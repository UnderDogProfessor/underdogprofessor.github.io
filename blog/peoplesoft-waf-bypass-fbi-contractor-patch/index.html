Incident/Incident breakdown/October 6, 2026

They encoded one letter

Oracle patched CVE-2026-35273 on June 10 and CISA set a June 15 deadline. The FBI's HR platform was breached on September 22, past a WAF rule.

SHREYAS LIPARE · GCIH · CEH MASTER

Anyone who has ever shipped a WAF rule to buy time on a patch, and anyone whose patching is performed by somebody they do not employ.

Researched and drafted with AI assistance, then reviewed and edited by Shreyas Lipare before publication. Every source below was checked against the original.

The character %50 is a capital P.

That is the whole trick. Organisations that could not patch Oracle PeopleSoft quickly blocked the vulnerable endpoint at the edge instead, with a rule matching the path /PSEMHUB/. The attacker started requesting /%50SEMHUB/ and the rules stopped matching [4].

On October 5, 2026 the FBI removed an Accenture contractor over a breach of its own systems, and said the cause was a patch that was issued and never installed [5]. The patch had been available since June.

What happened

Oracle published an out-of-band Security Alert on June 10, 2026 for CVE-2026-35273 in PeopleSoft Enterprise PeopleTools, versions 8.61 and 8.62 [1]. Oracle’s own description of it is short and sufficient: the flaw “is remotely exploitable without authentication” and “may result in remote code execution” [1]. The affected component is the Environment Management Hub, reached over HTTP, and Oracle scores it 9.8 [1].

CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on June 12 with a remediation date of June 15, and flagged it as associated with known ransomware use [2].

Then, in late September, two things happened in the same week. Mandiant and Google Threat Intelligence Group published that the activity cluster they track as UNC6240, which they associate with the name ShinyHunters, had resumed mass exploitation with a modified request designed to get past WAF rules [4]. And the FBI’s jobs portal was breached, with data on bureau employees taken.

Why this matters

Compare this with almost any other vulnerability story and the difference is that nothing was missing. Every warning that exists was issued, and issued early.

Oracle broke its own quarterly cadence to ship an alert out of band, which vendors do not do casually. CISA listed it inside two days and gave federal agencies three. The catalog entry carries the ransomware flag, which is the strongest signal in that dataset. EPSS has it in the 95th percentile [6]. A breach of a federal HR platform followed ninety nine days after the deadline.

So the interesting question is not whether anyone knew. It is what people did with knowing, and the answer for a meaningful number of organisations was to put a rule in front of the problem rather than fix it.

That decision is more defensible than it sounds. Patching an ERP platform means a change window, a regression test cycle and a conversation with whoever owns payroll. A WAF rule takes an afternoon. Faced with a June 15 deadline on a system that takes six weeks to patch properly, blocking the path is the move that gets you to the weekend.

The bet inside that decision is the part worth naming. A string-matching rule assumes the attacker keeps sending the same string.

Technical breakdown

The flaw itself needs little explanation. CISA’s catalog entry describes it as a missing authentication for critical function issue allowing an unauthenticated attacker to obtain takeover [2], and the NVD vector is the maximally bad one: network reachable, low complexity, no privileges, no user interaction [3]. There is no cleverness required to exploit it, which is why it has been used at scale since June.

The interesting engineering is in the bypass, and it is a disagreement about when decoding happens.

Mandiant describes it precisely: “The threat actor bypassed these string-based WAF rules by URL-encoding a single character in the request path, requesting /%50SEMHUB/ in place of /PSEMHUB/. Many WAF and reverse proxy rules match the literal path before URL decoding, while the PeopleSoft application server decodes the request and routes it to the vulnerable servlet” [4].

Two components in the same request path hold different opinions about what the request says. The rule inspects the raw string and sees a path it does not recognise. The application decodes the string and routes it to the endpoint the rule was written to protect. Neither is malfunctioning. They are simply answering different questions, and the gap between the answers is the exposure.

This generalises well past PeopleSoft, which is why it is worth your attention even if you have never run it. Any control that matches text before the application normalises it inherits this property. Mandiant’s guidance says so explicitly: assume “any percent-encoded, mixed-case, or otherwise non-normalized variant” and enforce blocking on the normalised path [4].

I have deliberately not built an attack chain for this post. The sequence from request to takeover is two steps long and the argument here is an interval rather than a route, which is what the timeline above carries.

What defenders should do Monday morning

Immediate, today. If you run PeopleTools 8.61 or 8.62, apply the Oracle Security Alert patch. Mandiant’s first line of remediation guidance is the cleanest statement of the lesson available: WAF rules and path-based blocking are not a substitute for patching [4]. If the Environment Management Hub is not needed in your deployment, Mandiant’s second item is to disable or remove it [4].

Near term, this week. Hunt. Mandiant publishes what to search for, and these are detection artefacts rather than anything offensive:

PIA WebLogic access logs   /PSEMHUB/ and percent-encoded variants (/%50SEMHUB/)
                           POST to /hub with bodies from external sources
                           unexpected .jsp or .jspx under PSEMHUB or PORTAL
Host telemetry             shell processes spawned by the WebLogic Java process
                           particularly invoking base64 -d, curl, /dev/tcp,
                           tasklist, or start /b

Rotate credentials readable by the PeopleSoft application service account, which is Mandiant’s fifth item and the one most often skipped after a scare [4].

Structural, this quarter. Separate doing the patch from verifying the patch. This is the part the FBI story is actually about. The bureau’s statement says the incident resulted from “a security failure of a platform managed by a third-party organization”, and specifically that it followed “a contractor failed to implement a security patch explicitly issued to secure the platform” [5]. Accenture’s public response was that it is “proud to support the mission of the FBI and will continue to do so”, and it declined to discuss the patching failure [5].

Pick one internet-facing system that somebody else patches on your behalf. Work out how you would prove today that the last critical advisory was applied, and by what date. If the answer is that you would ask them, you do not have verification, you have a relationship.

Checklist

  • PeopleTools 8.61 and 8.62 inventoried and patched for CVE-2026-35273.
  • Environment Management Hub disabled or removed where it is not required.
  • Any path-blocking rule rewritten to match the normalised path, not the literal string.
  • WebLogic access logs searched for /PSEMHUB/ and encoded variants.
  • Alerting on shell processes spawned by the WebLogic Java process.
  • Credentials readable by the PeopleSoft service account rotated.
  • One third-party-patched system chosen, and its verification path written down.

A compensating control is a bet, and somebody should know the odds

The phrase doing the damage here is “mitigated”. It gets written in a risk register, it closes a ticket, and it means something much weaker than the person reading it later will assume. The exposure did not go away in June. It was covered with a rule that matched a string, and three months later somebody changed the string.

I do not think the teams that chose the WAF rule were wrong to want one. The patch cycle for a payroll platform is genuinely painful, and a control you can ship in an afternoon against a three day federal deadline is a reasonable thing to reach for. What goes wrong is that the temporary measure is recorded in the same column as the permanent one, and then nobody returns.

The outsourcing half makes it worse in a specific way. When patching belongs to a supplier, the task leaves your organisation and the consequence does not, and very few arrangements put anybody in the middle whose job is to check. The FBI’s own review found exactly that gap. The uncomfortable part is how ordinary the arrangement is.

Go and look at what your register means by mitigated. In at least one row, it will mean a rule that matches a string.

FREQUENTLY ASKED

If we have a WAF rule blocking the vulnerable path, are we covered?
Not reliably, and this campaign is the demonstration. Mandiant's guidance is direct: WAF rules and path-based blocking are not a substitute for patching. The useful version of the control is to block on the normalised path rather than the literal string, and to assume percent-encoded, mixed-case or otherwise non-normalised variants will be tried. Even then it buys time rather than closing the hole.
Why does encoding one character defeat a rule at all?
Because two components disagree about when decoding happens. Many WAF and reverse proxy rules match the literal request path before URL decoding, while the application server decodes it and routes on the result. The request that reaches the application is therefore not the string the rule inspected. That gap is a property of the architecture rather than a bug in any particular product.
Was this a zero-day when the FBI was hit?
No. Oracle published an out-of-band Security Alert on June 10, 2026, and CISA added the flaw to its exploited catalog two days later with a June 15 remediation date. The breach of the jobs portal is dated September 22. There was a patch, and there had been for over three months.
What is UNC6240?
It is Mandiant's designation for the activity cluster behind this campaign, which it associates with the name ShinyHunters. Cluster designations describe observed activity rather than a confirmed organisation, so it is worth keeping the vendor label and the popular name distinct when you write this up internally.
We outsource patching. What should change on Monday?
Separate the doing from the verifying. A contract that assigns patching to a supplier does not transfer the consequence, and in most arrangements nobody is tasked with independently confirming that a specific advisory was applied by a specific date. Pick one internet-facing system a third party patches and establish how you would prove, today, that the last critical advisory was actioned.
CONFIDENCE AND LIMITATIONS

Dates, scores and catalog entries come from Oracle's own Security Alert, the NVD record and CISA's KEV feed at catalog version 2026.10.04, all read on October 6, 2026, and I re-ran the KEV check that morning. The encoding bypass and the hunting guidance are Mandiant's, published September 25, 2026. The contractor's removal and the FBI's statement come from Reuters reporting of October 5, 2026, which cites sources rather than a published bureau release, so treat the surrounding detail as credible reporting rather than a primary document. Two things I want to be explicit about. The claim that this specific flaw was the route into the FBI's portal originates with the attacker; what the FBI has confirmed is narrower, that a contractor failed to implement a patch issued to secure a third-party managed platform, and Reuters notes it could not confirm the attacker's account of the entry route. And I have no information on whether the FBI's own instance was among those running a WAF rule in place of the patch, so the bypass explains how that substitution failed generally and is not established as the mechanism in this case.
